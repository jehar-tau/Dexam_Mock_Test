The NIFT CAT is a paper-based test of observation, concept, colour and illustration. Many students practise illustration and colour for months, then freeze when a question hands them a theme and asks for an idea. The drawing is ready, but the concept isn't.

Concept is not a mysterious gift. It is the result of a process you can learn and repeat under time pressure. This article walks you through that process and gives you drills to make it automatic.

## What a concept is, and isn't

A concept is the central idea behind your response: the angle you choose, the story you tell or the message you want the viewer to take away. It is not the same as the subject. If the theme is "rain", the subject is rain. A concept might be "rain as the city's alarm clock", or "the one dry spot under a shared umbrella".

Good concepts tend to share a few qualities:

- **They are specific.** One clear situation, not a general collage of everything related to the theme.
- **They are personal or unexpected.** They come from noticing something others might miss.
- **They are readable.** A viewer can understand the idea without a long explanation.
- **They are drawable.** You can actually show it well in the time and space you have.

## The five-step path

Use this path for every theme. With practice it takes a few minutes, leaving the rest of your time for execution. Check the year's brochure and admit card for the actual time and instructions, and plan your split around them.

### 1. Unpack the theme (2–3 minutes)

Write the theme in the centre of a rough area of the page. Around it, write every word that comes to mind in four directions:

- **Literal:** objects, places and people directly linked to the theme.
- **Feelings:** emotions the theme brings up.
- **Opposites and contrasts:** what the theme is not, or what it fights against.
- **Personal:** your own memories, your town, your family, your daily routine.

Don't judge. Write fast. Aim for at least fifteen words.

### 2. Find the unexpected link (2 minutes)

Look for two words from different directions that make an interesting pair. A literal word with a feeling, or a contrast with a personal memory. That pairing is often where an original idea lives.

Ask yourself: "What would most people draw for this theme?" Then deliberately set that aside, or twist it.

### 3. Thumbnail three ideas (5 minutes)

Draw three tiny, rough versions of different ideas. Each should be the size of a matchbox. Use quick marks for figures and objects. You are testing whether the idea can be shown, not making art yet.

Under each thumbnail, write one line that sums up the idea. If you can't write the line, the idea isn't clear yet.

### 4. Choose and plan (2–3 minutes)

Pick the thumbnail that is clearest and most original. Then decide:

- **Focal point:** what the viewer should see first.
- **Viewpoint:** eye level, from above, from below, close up.
- **Composition:** where the main subject sits on the page, and what fills the background.
- **Colour plan:** a limited palette that supports the mood.

### 5. Execute

Lightly block in the composition, then the main subject, then supporting elements, then colour, then details. Keep the focal point the most finished and most contrasted part of the page. If the question asks for a title or a short write-up, keep it brief and make sure it matches what you drew.

## Using colour to carry the idea

Colour is one of the things the CAT tests, so treat it as part of the concept, not decoration added at the end.

- **Pick a limited palette.** Two or three main colours plus a neutral usually read better than every colour in the box.
- **Match colour to mood.** Warm colours often feel lively, close or tense. Cool colours often feel calm, distant or quiet. Muted colours suggest memory or tiredness.
- **Use contrast to lead the eye.** Put your strongest contrast, in colour or in light and dark, at the focal point.
- **Decide on the light.** Is it a harsh afternoon, a soft evening, a tube-light indoors? Let that decision guide every colour choice.

Practise with whatever medium you plan to use in the exam, and check this year's instructions for any rules on materials.

## Clichés and how to escape them

Some ideas appear again and again, and they make a response easy to forget. For common themes, write down the first idea that comes to mind, then ask:

1. Can I move the idea to a place I know well, like my street, my school or my kitchen?
2. Can I show it from an unusual point of view, like a pet's, a child's or an object's?
3. Can I show the moment just before or just after the obvious moment?
4. Can I zoom in to one small detail instead of the big picture?

Any of these twists can turn a familiar idea into your own.

## Drills to build concept speed

### Theme cards (10 minutes a day)

Write fifty simple themes on small cards: everyday words like "waiting", "noise", "home", "speed", "festival", "repair", "shadow", "tomorrow". Each day, draw one card and run steps 1 to 3 only: word web, link, three thumbnails. Stop after ten minutes. Keep all the pages.

### Full sprints (twice a week)

Twice a week, run the full five steps on one theme, including colour, with a timer. Start generous, then shorten the time as you improve.

### Concept review (once a week)

Spread out your week's thumbnails. For each one, ask: Is it specific? Is it unexpected? Would a stranger understand it? Mark your three strongest and note what they have in common. That tells you how your best ideas tend to arise.

### Observation feeding concept

Concept depends on observation. Carry a small notebook and record small moments: a vendor's arrangement of fruit, people sheltering from sun, a child copying an adult. These moments become raw material when a theme needs a personal angle.

## A quick checklist before you finish

- Does the drawing clearly respond to the theme given?
- Is there one clear idea, not several competing ones?
- Can the viewer find the focal point in a second?
- Does the colour support the mood of the idea?
- Is the page used well, with a balanced composition?
- If a title or write-up is needed, does it match the drawing?
