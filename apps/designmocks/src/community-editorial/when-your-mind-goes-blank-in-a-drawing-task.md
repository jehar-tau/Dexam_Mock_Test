You turn to the drawing task, read the prompt, and your mind goes completely empty. The clock is running. Everyone around you seems to be drawing already. The harder you try to think of an idea, the emptier it feels.

This happens to many students, including ones who draw well at home. It is one of the most common worries about the drawing parts of design entrance exams, whether that is the drawing and design aptitude tasks in UCEED Part B, the sketching and design tasks in CEED Part B, the Creative Ability Test in NIFT, or the visual prompts in NID DAT Prelims.

The good news is that a blank is not a lack of ability. It usually comes from pressure and from waiting for the one perfect idea. If you have a method ready, you can work your way out of it in a few minutes.

## Why blanks happen

Under pressure, we tend to judge ideas before they have fully formed. An idea appears, a voice says "too simple" or "everyone will draw that", and it disappears. After a few rounds of this, it feels as though no ideas are coming at all.

The fix is to separate two jobs: **producing** ideas and **choosing** one. Produce first, quickly and without judging. Choose afterwards.

## The first minute: stop, breathe, re-read

When the blank hits, do not stare at the page hoping. Do this instead:

1. Put your pencil down.
2. Take two or three slow breaths, with the out-breath longer than the in-breath.
3. Re-read the prompt slowly, word by word.
4. Underline the key words: the subject, the action, any limits such as a viewpoint, a number of objects or a mood.

Very often, a careful second reading shows you something you missed the first time, and that detail becomes your starting point.

## Break the prompt into words

Take the corner of your rough sheet, if one is allowed, or the margin, and write as many related words as you can in two minutes. Do not judge them. Aim for ten or more.

Say the prompt is about "a moment of waiting". Your list might include: bus stop, hospital corridor, queue, clock, rain, phone screen, a dog at a door, a pot about to boil, results day, a seed in soil.

Now you have ten possible scenes instead of none. Some will be ordinary. One or two will be interesting. That is all you need.

## Ask five quick questions

If the word list is slow, ask yourself these questions about the prompt:

- **Who** is in this scene? A child, an old person, an animal, a crowd?
- **Where** is it happening? Indoors, outdoors, a city, a village, somewhere unusual?
- **When?** Morning, night, a festival, during rain?
- **What is happening** at this exact moment?
- **What is unusual** about it? What would make someone look twice?

The last question is often where a memorable idea comes from. An everyday scene with one surprising detail can be stronger than a complicated fantasy.

## Get your hand moving

Thinking alone can keep you stuck. Drawing small, rough sketches often unlocks ideas faster than thinking about them.

Draw three or four small boxes and give yourself about a minute for each. Inside each box, sketch a quick thumbnail of a different idea from your list: just shapes, a horizon line, a figure or two. These are not for the examiner. They are for you.

Bad thumbnails are completely fine. Their job is to get you out of your head and onto the page.

## Keep a fallback toolkit

Before the exam, practise a few dependable ways to turn any prompt into a scene. If nothing comes, reach for one:

- **Change the viewpoint.** Show the scene from above, from ground level or through a window.
- **Change the scale.** Show the scene from an ant's point of view, or zoom right into one detail.
- **Show before and after.** Split the page or suggest what has just happened.
- **Combine two things.** Bring two unrelated words from your list together and see what happens.
- **Go everyday.** Place the prompt in a setting you know well: your kitchen, your street, your school corridor.

It also helps to have practised drawing a few things you are likely to need often, such as hands, people sitting and walking, a street with simple perspective, and common objects. When you know you can draw these, your mind is freer to think about the idea.

## Choose one and commit

After a few minutes of producing ideas, stop and choose. A useful test is: **Which idea can I make clearest to someone seeing it for the first time?** Clarity usually matters more than complexity.

Then commit. Do not switch ideas halfway unless something is clearly not working. Constant switching uses up your time and leaves you with nothing finished.

Plan your time before you begin. UCEED Part B, for example, gives you 60 minutes for two tasks, and CEED Part B gives you 120 minutes for five, so you know roughly how much each task can take. For other exams, check the paper's instructions. A simple split for one task is a short time for ideas and thumbnails, most of the time for the main drawing, and a few minutes at the end to check the prompt again and tidy up.

## If you blank halfway through

Sometimes the idea is fine but you get stuck while drawing. If that happens:

- Step back and look at the whole page for a few seconds
- Ask what would help a viewer understand the scene: a person, a background, a light source, a shadow
- Re-read the prompt and check you are still answering it
- If the task asks for a short note or explanation, write it now. Putting your idea into words can show you what the drawing still needs.

## Practise blanking on purpose

The best way to handle blanks in the exam is to rehearse them at home. A few times a week:

1. Ask someone to give you a random prompt, or write prompts on slips of paper and pick one without looking.
2. Give yourself two minutes for a word list and three minutes for thumbnails.
3. Choose one and draw it under a timer.

After a few weeks, the steps become automatic. When the blank comes in the exam, your hand will already know what to do.

If worry about drawing tasks is making you dread practice or lose sleep, talk to a parent, a teacher or a counsellor. Many students feel this way, and sharing it usually makes it lighter.

## Try this week

- Write five practice prompts on slips of paper and do the two-minute word list for each.
- Practise the "stop, breathe, re-read, underline" routine before every drawing session.
- Do three-box thumbnail drills for at least three prompts.
- Pick two fallback tools, such as changing the viewpoint and going everyday, and use each once.
- Time one full drawing task for your exam and note how you split the minutes.
