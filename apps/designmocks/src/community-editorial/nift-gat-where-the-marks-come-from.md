Students often ask which part of the NIFT GAT "gives the most marks". It is the right instinct, but the answer is not one section. It is a way of reading the paper. Once you see how the GAT is scored, you can work out where *your* marks come from, which matters far more than where anyone else's do.

## The facts that shape everything

The NIFT B.Des. GAT is computer-based and runs for 120 minutes. It has 100 single-answer MCQs. A right answer earns +1, a wrong answer costs −0.25 and a blank earns 0.

Three things follow from that.

1. **Every question is worth the same.** The hardest question in the paper earns exactly what the easiest one earns.
2. **Your average budget is 72 seconds per question.** That is 120 minutes divided by 100 questions.
3. **Wrong answers cost a quarter mark each.** Four wrong answers cancel one right one.

The section names and how many questions each section has are set out in this year's brochure. Check them there, because the split can change from year to year.

## The score equation

Your GAT score is simply:

**Score = number right − 0.25 × number wrong**

Here are two made-up candidates to show the arithmetic. These are not targets, just numbers to compare.

- Candidate A attempts 90 questions and gets 70 right and 20 wrong. Score: 70 − 5 = 65.
- Candidate B attempts 75 questions and gets 68 right and 7 wrong. Score: 68 − 1.75 = 66.25.

B attempted 15 fewer questions and still scored more. The lesson is not "attempt fewer". It is that careless wrong answers quietly eat your score, and that accuracy and speed have to be trained together.

## Fast marks and slow marks

Since every mark is equal, the real question is: how many marks can I collect per minute? Sort question types into three groups as you practise:

- **Fast:** you can answer in under 30 seconds with confidence.
- **Workable:** about a minute, with a clear method.
- **Slow:** two minutes or more, or no clear method yet.

A slow question earns the same single mark as a fast one. So on your first run through the paper, the fast and workable ones come first. Slow questions wait until everything quicker is done.

This is personal. A question type that is slow for one student is fast for another. Your groups will come from your own mocks, not from a list someone else made.

## Build your own marks map

This is the most useful exercise in this article. Take one full GAT-style paper and sit it under real conditions. Then, for each section, record five numbers in your notebook:

1. Questions in the section.
2. Questions you attempted.
3. Questions right.
4. Questions wrong.
5. Minutes you spent.

Now work out your **net marks per minute** for each section:

**(right − 0.25 × wrong) ÷ minutes spent**

Say you got 18 right and 2 wrong in a section and spent 20 minutes on it. Net marks are 18 − 0.5 = 17.5, so your rate is 17.5 ÷ 20 = 0.875 marks per minute.

Compare the rates. Your highest-rate sections are where your easiest marks are. Your lowest-rate sections tell you what to study, or which questions to leave for later in the paper. Redo the map after every two or three mocks. It will change as you improve, and that change is a better measure of progress than your raw score.

## A 120-minute plan

Here is a starting plan to adjust after a few mocks. Check this year's instructions and the mock interface to see whether you can move freely between sections.

1. **Minutes 0 to 75: first pass.** Go through the whole paper. Answer fast and workable questions. If a question has no clear path after about 60 seconds, mark it and move on.
2. **Minutes 75 to 110: second pass.** Return to the marked questions and solve the ones you now can.
3. **Minutes 110 to 120: final decisions.** For remaining questions, apply your guessing rule. Then stop. Do not spend the last minutes second-guessing answers you were sure of.

Write the three clock times on your rough sheet as soon as the test starts.

## When to guess in the GAT

The break-even chance in GAT is 0.25 ÷ 1.25 = 0.2, one in five. Check how many options each question has in this year's paper. If there are four:

- A blind guess is worth 1 × 1/4 − 0.25 × 3/4, about +0.06 marks on average.
- Ruling out one option makes it 1 × 1/3 − 0.25 × 2/3, about +0.17.
- Ruling out two makes it 1 × 1/2 − 0.25 × 1/2, about +0.38.

If there were five options, a blind guess would be worth exactly zero.

So a blind guess gains you almost nothing, while ruling out an option makes attempting clearly worthwhile. My rule for GAT: **attempt when you can rule out at least one option, and spend your time on questions you can solve rather than on guesses.**

## Practise in GAT-sized blocks

Full papers are tiring, and you do not need one every day. A good everyday drill is a block of 25 questions in 30 minutes. That is exactly 72 seconds per question, the real pace.

- Mix question types in each block, the way the real paper mixes them.
- Keep a running tally of right, wrong and blank, and work out net marks.
- At the end, list any question that took over two minutes, and note whether it was worth it.

Four blocks make a full paper's worth of questions without the fatigue. Save full timed papers for once a week or so.

## Keep the CAT in view

The GAT is not the whole NIFT B.Des. picture. The CAT is a separate paper-based test of observation, concept, colour and illustration. Check this year's brochure for how GAT and CAT are weighted in your final score and plan your weekly hours to match. A balanced week usually includes both: GAT blocks on some days, drawing and colour work on others.
