Students often ask how to "improve observation", as if it were a talent you either have or don't. It isn't. Observation is a habit of paying attention on purpose, and habits grow with small, regular practice. Ten focused minutes a day will do more for you than one long session on a Sunday.

This article gives you a daily routine, a bank of drills to rotate through, and a simple way to check that you are actually getting better.

## Why observation matters in design exams

Every design entrance test asks you to notice things. You may be shown an image and asked what is different, what is missing or what comes next. You may be asked to draw a scene from memory. The NIFT CAT, for example, tests observation directly alongside concept, colour and illustration. In UCEED and CEED Part A, many visual questions reward the student who looks carefully before answering, because the wrong options are often built to catch a hurried glance.

Good observation also feeds your drawing. When you have really looked at how a chair leg meets the seat, or how a shadow falls from a bottle, you can draw it convincingly without a reference.

## The ten-minute routine

Do this once a day, at the same time if you can. You need a pencil, a small notebook and a timer.

1. **Pick one subject (30 seconds).** Choose something within reach: a shoe, a pressure cooker, a corner of your room, the view from a window. Don't overthink the choice.
2. **Look without drawing (2 minutes).** Study it in silence. Notice its overall shape first, then the parts, then the small details: joints, edges, textures, labels, wear marks.
3. **Turn away and draw from memory (3 minutes).** Put the object out of sight, or turn your back to it. Draw everything you remember. Add quick notes for things you can't draw well, such as "rough texture" or "faded red".
4. **Compare (3 minutes).** Look at the object again. With a different colour pen, circle what you got wrong and add what you missed. Be honest. The circles are the whole point.
5. **Write one line (1 minute).** At the bottom of the page, write the single biggest thing you missed. For example: "I forgot the handle is lower on one side."
6. **Close the book (30 seconds).** Date the page and stop. Stopping on time keeps the habit light enough to repeat tomorrow.

After a week, read your one-line notes together. You will usually see a pattern. Some people always miss proportions. Others miss small text, or the number of repeated parts. That pattern tells you what to train next.

## A bank of drills to rotate

Once the routine feels easy, swap step 3 for one of these drills on some days. Each takes about the same time.

### Counting and checking

Look at a busy shelf, a crowded photo or a pile of utensils for 60 seconds. Then write down how many of each type of thing there were. Check. Counting forces you to scan systematically instead of letting your eyes wander.

### Spot the change

Ask someone at home to move, remove or swap two or three small things on a table while you look away. Then find the changes. If you practise alone, take a photo of a scene, change something, and compare the scene with the photo a few hours later.

### Light and shadow

Place an object near a single light source, such as a window or a lamp. For two minutes, note only the light: where it is brightest, where the darkest shadow sits, where reflected light softens the shadow, and what shape the cast shadow takes on the floor. Then sketch only the tones, with no outlines.

### Sizes and ratios

Estimate before you measure. How many times taller is the door than it is wide? How many of your hands fit across the table? Write your guess, then check with a ruler or your hand. Proportion is where memory drawings often go wrong, and this drill trains your eye to judge it.

### Street reading

When you are out, pick one category for the whole trip: shop signs, footwear, auto-rickshaw decorations, railings, or packaging in a kirana shop. Notice as many variations as you can. Back home, sketch five from memory.

### Colour naming

Pick an object and describe its colours in words more precise than "blue" or "brown". Is it a dusty blue, a greyish blue, a blue that turns green in shadow? Precise words make you look harder, and they help when an exam asks you to work with colour.

## How to look, step by step

When you study any subject, move through it in the same order every time. A fixed order stops you from skipping things.

- **Big shape first.** Is it tall, wide, round, boxy? What simple shape could hold it?
- **Proportions next.** How does the height compare with the width? Where is the middle?
- **Parts and joints.** How many parts are there? How do they connect?
- **Surfaces.** Matte or shiny, smooth or rough, new or worn?
- **Light.** Where does it come from, and where are the shadows?
- **Details last.** Text, screws, stitching, patterns, small marks.

Say the steps in your head as you go. After a couple of weeks, the order becomes automatic, and you will use it in the exam without thinking.

## Common mistakes

- **Drawing while looking.** In this routine, the looking and the drawing are separate. Drawing while you look trains copying, not memory.
- **Only choosing easy objects.** Mix simple objects with complex ones. A steel tumbler is a good start, a cycle is a good challenge.
- **Skipping the compare step.** Without comparing, you never find out what you missed, and you repeat the same gaps.
- **Doing too much at once.** One subject per day is enough. Ten minutes done daily beats an hour done once.
- **Judging the drawing quality.** This is an observation exercise. Scribbles and labels are fine as long as the information is right.

## Checking your progress

Every Sunday, spend five extra minutes on a quick test. Choose an object you have never used before. Look for two minutes, draw from memory for three, then compare. Count how many things you circled as wrong or missing and write that number on the page.

Don't expect the number to fall in a straight line. Harder objects will push it up. But across a month, you should notice that you remember more parts, get proportions closer, and catch the small details more often. Keep the Sunday pages together so you can flip through them and see the difference.

## Taking it into the exam

In an objective paper, slow down on visual questions for the first few seconds. Look at the whole figure before you look at the options, using the same big-shape-to-details order. Then check each option against the figure, one feature at a time. In UCEED and CEED, single-answer MCQs carry negative marking, so a careful second look before you click is worth the time.

In a drawing task, use what you have stored. When you need to draw a scene from memory, start with the big shapes and proportions you have practised, then add the details you trained yourself to notice.

## Try this week

- Do the ten-minute routine on at least five days, with one new object each day.
- Swap in two drills from the bank, such as counting and light and shadow.
- On Sunday, read your one-line notes and name your most common gap.
- Choose one category for street reading and sketch five examples from memory.
- Solve a set of visual questions from a year-wise paper on DMocks, looking at the full figure before the options.
