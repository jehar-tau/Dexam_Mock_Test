Spatial questions are the ones many students skip on sight. A folded net, a stack of blocks, a shape rotated to an odd angle: they look like pure instinct, so people assume they either "get it" or they don't. In fact, most of these questions can be solved with a few reliable rules and a lot of handled practice.

This article covers nets, dice, painted cubes, block counting and mental rotation, and ends with a weekly drill plan.

## Start with your hands, not your head

Before you try to rotate anything in your mind, rotate real things. Your brain learns spatial patterns much faster when your hands are involved.

- Make a paper cube from a net. Number the faces 1 to 6 and colour opposite faces the same colour.
- Use a real die if you have one. Notice which numbers are opposite and which share an edge.
- Stack a few matchboxes, sugar cubes or erasers into small shapes. Draw them from the front, side and top.

Spend the first few days of practice with these objects on your desk. When a question confuses you, build it. Over time you will need the model less and less.

## Nets of a cube

A net is the flat pattern that folds into a solid. A cube has eleven different nets. You don't need to memorise them all, but you do need a fast way to tell which faces end up opposite each other.

### Rules for opposite faces

1. **In a straight row of three squares, the first and third are opposite.** The middle one sits between them.
2. **Two faces that share an edge in the net are never opposite.** They will be neighbours on the cube.
3. **Each face has exactly one opposite.** Once you find five of the pairings, the last one is fixed.

### A step-by-step check

When a question shows a net and asks which cube it makes:

1. Find all three opposite pairs using the rules above. Write them down, such as "A–D, B–F, C–E".
2. Look at each answer option. If any option shows two opposite faces side by side, eliminate it at once.
3. For the options that remain, check the direction of any arrows, dots or patterns on the faces. Fold the net in your mind one face at a time, keeping one face fixed as the base.
4. If two options still look right, check the corner where three faces meet. In a correct cube, the order of those three faces around the corner must match the net.

That elimination in step 2 often removes most of the wrong options before you have to imagine any folding.

## Dice and cube positions

Some questions show the same die or cube in two or three positions and ask what is opposite a given face.

1. **Look for a face that appears in two views.** Use it as an anchor.
2. **List the faces seen next to it.** Every face that touches the anchor in any view cannot be its opposite.
3. **What is left over is the opposite.** With four neighbours known, the sixth face is the answer.

When two views share two faces, compare their order going clockwise around the shared corner. That order tells you where the remaining faces must sit.

## Painted cubes

A classic question: a large cube is painted on all sides, then cut into smaller equal cubes. How many small cubes have paint on three faces, two faces, one face or none?

If the large cube is cut into n small cubes along each edge, so n × n × n in total, then:

- **Three faces painted:** 8. These are the corners, and a cube always has eight.
- **Two faces painted:** 12 × (n − 2). These sit along the twelve edges, minus the corners.
- **One face painted:** 6 × (n − 2)². These are in the middle of each of the six faces.
- **No faces painted:** (n − 2)³. These are hidden inside.

Check your memory with n = 3: you get 8, 12, 6 and 1, which add up to 27. Do this check every time, because the four counts must always add up to n³.

Don't just learn the formulas. Picture each group. The corners, the edges, the face centres and the hidden core. When a question changes the setup, such as painting only some faces or using a cuboid, picturing the groups lets you adapt.

## Counting blocks in a stack

For a drawing of stacked cubes, count in layers or columns, never by staring at the whole.

1. Count column by column from the top view, if one is given. Write the height of each column in a small grid.
2. If only one view is given, count layer by layer from the top, and remember that some cubes must exist underneath to support those above.
3. Add the numbers. Check for hidden cubes behind or below visible ones.

## Mental rotation

Some questions show a shape and ask which option is the same shape rotated, and which is a mirror image. These rules help:

- **Track two features, not the whole shape.** Pick a distinctive part, like a notch or a short arm, and a second one. Rotate those two in your mind and check where they land.
- **Rotate one step at a time.** Turn by 90 degrees, check, then turn again. Large rotations in one go are where errors creep in.
- **Watch for mirror images.** A shape and its mirror image can't be matched by rotating on the page. If the order of features goes clockwise in the original and anticlockwise in the option, it is a reflection.
- **Use your hand.** Hold your hand in the shape's orientation and turn it. It sounds silly, but it works.

## Handling these questions in the exam

Spatial questions can be slow, so plan for them. In UCEED, single-answer MCQs carry −0.71 for a wrong answer; in CEED, −0.5. Multiple-select questions in both exams carry −1 for any wrong option. Numerical-answer questions have no penalty.

- If you can eliminate options using quick rules, such as opposite faces in a net, answer confidently.
- If you are stuck between options after a fair attempt, mark the question for review and come back.
- For multiple-select questions, only mark options you are sure of, since partial credit rewards a correct subset and one wrong option costs you.

## A weekly drill plan

- **Monday:** 20 minutes with a paper cube and a set of net questions.
- **Tuesday:** 20 minutes of dice and cube-position questions.
- **Wednesday:** 15 minutes of painted-cube questions, including your own variations.
- **Thursday:** 15 minutes drawing front, side and top views of block stacks you build.
- **Friday:** 20 minutes of mental rotation and mirror-image questions.
- **Saturday:** a timed mixed set of 15 questions. Note the time spent on each.
- **Sunday:** review every mistake. Write down which rule would have caught it.

## Try this week

- Build a paper cube, number its faces and keep it on your desk.
- Learn the three rules for opposite faces and test them on ten nets.
- Work out the painted-cube counts for n = 3, 4 and 5, and check that each adds up to n³.
- Do one timed mixed set and review every error.
- Find the spatial questions in a year-wise paper on DMocks and solve them with the elimination steps above.
