A mock test gives you two things: a number and a pile of evidence. Most students read the number, feel good or bad about it, and move on to the next mock. The number is the least useful part. The evidence is where your next ten marks are hiding.

This is the review method I'd use after every mock, whether it is an objective paper or a drawing section.

## When to review

Review the same day or the next day. Within a day you still remember *why* you chose each answer, and that reasoning is what you are trying to fix. Leave it a week and you will only see what you chose, not why.

Set aside roughly as long as the test itself for an objective paper. A two-hour paper deserves close to two hours of review. That feels like a lot. It is still the best use of those hours, because a mock you do not review is mostly wasted.

For a drawing section, 30 to 45 minutes of review is usually enough.

## Step 1: Split your score into three numbers

Your total hides what really happened. Break it into:

1. **Marks earned.** Everything you got from correct answers and partial credit.
2. **Marks lost to penalties.** Every wrong answer that carried negative marking.
3. **Marks left on the table.** Questions you skipped or never reached, but could have solved with a calmer head or a little more time.

The penalty number is often a surprise. In UCEED, each wrong single-answer MCQ costs 0.71, so six wrong MCQs cost 6 × 0.71 = 4.26 marks, on top of the marks you did not earn. In NIFT GAT, each wrong answer costs 0.25, which means four wrong answers wipe out one right one.

Write all three numbers at the top of a fresh page. They tell you which way to push next. A big penalty number means slow down and guess less. A big "left on the table" number means speed up or fix your order.

## Step 2: Sort every imperfect question

Go through every question that was not a confident, correct answer. That includes the ones you got right by guessing, because luck does not repeat. Put each one in a single category:

- **Didn't know.** You were missing a skill or a fact.
- **Misread.** You answered a different question from the one asked. "Not", "except" and "all of the following" are common culprits.
- **Slip.** You knew how to do it but made an arithmetic error or clicked the wrong option.
- **Time.** You never reached it, or you rushed it in the last minutes.
- **Bad guess.** You guessed without being able to rule anything out, or you were overconfident.
- **Too slow.** You got it right but spent more than twice your average time on it.

Count each category. The biggest count is your main problem, and it is often not the one you expected.

## Step 3: Fill in the review template

For each question in your list, write five short lines in a notebook. Keep it brief: a review that takes too long to write will not happen after every mock.

- **Question and type:** for example, "Q23, mirror image of a 3D shape".
- **What I did:** one line on your actual thinking.
- **Category:** from Step 2.
- **The move I should have used:** the method, not the answer.
- **How I'll catch it next time:** a concrete habit, such as "circle the word *not* before reading the options".

The last line matters most. "Be more careful" is not a habit. "Re-read the question stem before clicking" is.

## Step 4: Look at your time

Accuracy is half the story. Time is the other half. If you can, note how long each section took and roughly when you passed each tenth question.

It helps to know your average budget per question:

- **UCEED Part A:** 120 minutes for 57 questions, a little over two minutes each.
- **CEED Part A:** 60 minutes for 44 questions, about 80 seconds each.
- **NIFT GAT:** 120 minutes for 100 questions, 72 seconds each.

Then look for *time sinks*: questions where you spent more than double the average. Mark each one as "worth it" (you got it right and it was a fair use of time) or "not worth it" (you got it wrong, or you could have taken two easier questions in that time). Those "not worth it" questions are what the two-pass method is designed to fix.

## Step 5: Review drawing tasks on their own terms

A drawing section has no answer key, so you review against the task instead. Put your sheet next to the question and check:

- Did I answer exactly what was asked, including every part of the prompt?
- Is the main idea readable from a distance, in a couple of seconds?
- Did I finish, or does one corner look abandoned?
- How long did I spend thinking, roughing out and finishing? Which phase ran over?
- If someone saw only my drawing, could they guess what the prompt was?

That last test is a good one to try with a family member or friend. You do not need a design teacher to find out whether your idea comes across.

Then compare with your previous mock's drawing. One improvement you can point to is worth more than a vague sense of "better".

## Step 6: Choose three changes, no more

Ten resolutions will not survive the next mock. Pick three:

1. **One knowledge fix.** A topic or question type to practise this week.
2. **One process fix.** A habit inside the exam, such as underlining negatives or marking questions for review sooner.
3. **One time fix.** A rule about pacing, such as "leave any question with no clear path after 90 seconds".

Write the three on a sticky note, or at the top of the rough sheet for your next mock. When the next review comes, check whether each one worked.

## Keep an error log across mocks

After three or four mocks, put your category counts side by side. Patterns appear that one mock cannot show. If "misread" keeps topping the list, more practice questions will not fix it. A slower reading habit will. If "time" keeps topping the list, your order or your skipping rule needs work, not your knowledge.

## What not to do

- Do not retake the same mock straight away. Your second score will mostly measure memory.
- Do not review only the questions you got wrong. Lucky guesses and slow right answers hide problems too.
- Do not compare your score with numbers strangers post online. You cannot know what paper they sat or how they counted.
- Do not skip the review because the score was good. Good scores have fixable leaks too.

## Try this week

- Take one mock under full exam conditions and review it within 24 hours.
- Split the score into earned, lost to penalties and left on the table.
- Sort every imperfect question into one of the six categories and count them.
- Fill in the five-line template for at least ten questions.
- Write down three changes and test them in your next mock.
