Old papers are the closest thing you have to the real exam. They show you the kinds of question the setters like, how the instructions are worded, how drawing prompts are framed and how tight the clock feels. That makes them precious. It also makes them easy to waste.

The usual trap goes like this. You solve a paper, check the answers, and a few days later you solve it again. Your score jumps. You feel ready. But most of that jump is memory. You remembered that the answer to the cube question was C, not *why* it was C. In the exam the cube will be different, and memory will not help you.

This article is about using each old paper so that what you keep is the method, not the answer.

## Why memorising feels like progress

When you recognise a question, you get a small rush of confidence. The feeling is real, but it measures familiarity, not skill. Here is a quick test. If someone changed one number, one shape or one word in the question, could you still solve it at the same speed? If not, you have memorised it.

Design entrance exams are good at punishing this. Visual reasoning, spatial questions, observation tasks and drawing prompts rarely come back in the same form. What comes back is the *type* of thinking. That is what you are hunting for.

## The first attempt is sacred

You only get one honest attempt at each paper. Protect it.

- Sit it under real conditions: the full time limit, one sitting, phone in another room.
- Do not look anything up halfway through.
- As you go, mark every question as sure, half-sure or guess. A tick, a dot and a question mark on your rough sheet are enough.
- Note the clock time when you finish each section or each drawing task.

That first score is your baseline. Write it down with the date. Every later attempt at the same paper is practice, not measurement, so never compare the two as if they meant the same thing.

## Review by type, not by answer

After the attempt, do not just tick right and wrong. Go through the paper and give every question a short *type* label in your own words. For example: "rotating a 3D object", "counting hidden faces", "odd one out by colour", "reading a scene for mood", "spotting the error in a diagram", "next shape in a sequence".

Then, under each question, write one line: *the move that solves it*. Not the answer. The move.

- "Follow one corner of the cube and ignore the rest."
- "Count edges first, then faces."
- "Read the last line of the question before the long setup."

After three or four papers your type list starts to repeat. That list is gold. It is a map of what the exam actually tests, built by you, from the source.

## Turn each question into three questions

This is the best single way to stop memorising. For every question you got wrong or guessed, invent two variations before you move on.

1. **Change one thing.** A different number, a different rotation, a different colour, a different object.
2. **Flip the direction.** If the question asked "which view is from the top", ask yourself "which object would give this top view".
3. **Solve your variations cold.** Do it without looking at the original solution.

If you can solve your own variations, you have learned the move. If you cannot, you only knew the answer.

Do the same with drawing and design prompts. Keep the structure of the task and change the subject. A prompt about a crowded railway platform becomes a crowded vegetable market, then a crowded school corridor. You practise the skill the prompt is testing, such as composition, perspective or telling a story in one frame, instead of rehearsing one drawing until you can repeat it.

## Space out repeat attempts

If you do want to re-solve a paper, wait. Three to four weeks is a sensible minimum. Before you start, cover up whatever you wrote last time.

When you re-solve, change the goal. The first time, the goal was a score. The second time, the goal is speed and explanation.

- Time yourself per question, not just per paper.
- Say the move out loud before you choose an answer.
- If you "just know" an answer but cannot say why, mark it. That is memory talking. Count it as wrong when you review.

## Keep a move notebook, not an answer notebook

Many students keep notebooks full of solved questions. Those notebooks turn into a second memory test. Keep a *move notebook* instead. Each page is one question type, and under it you list the moves that worked, in your own words, with a tiny sketch if it helps.

A page might read like this:

> **Rotating 3D objects.** Pick one marked face and follow only that face. Check the hidden side last. If two options look identical, look for a mirror image.

Read your move notebook the evening before a mock. Leave the solved questions alone.

## Learn the exam's habits too

Old papers also teach you how the exam behaves as a whole. On your first look at a new paper, before you solve anything, spend five minutes noting:

- How long are the instructions, and which parts are easy to misread?
- Which question types appear early, and which appear late?
- For drawing tasks, what do the prompts ask for besides the drawing? A title, a short written note, a set number of frames, a particular view?
- What looks different compared with the previous year's paper?

Formats change from year to year. Treat old papers as evidence of style, not a promise about this year's pattern. Check this year's brochure for the current format before you plan your time.

## How many old papers do you need?

There is no magic number. A better question is: how many papers until your type list stops growing? When two papers in a row add no new types, you have probably mapped the exam well. After that, use the remaining papers as full timed mocks, spaced out, closer to the exam.

If you only have a few papers, ration them. Keep at least two untouched for the final weeks so you still have honest, unseen practice when it matters most.

## A simple weekly rhythm

If you are preparing alone, this rhythm is a good place to start:

1. One full old paper, timed, in a single sitting.
2. A review session the next day, by type, with the three-question trick for every miss.
3. Two short sessions during the week on your weakest types, using your own variations.
4. One evening reading your move notebook.

That is four or five sessions, not the whole week. It leaves room for drawing practice and for rest.

## Try this week

- Sit one old paper you have never seen under full exam conditions, marking each answer as sure, half-sure or guess.
- Label every question with a type and write the move under it, not the answer.
- Pick three questions you got wrong, invent two variations of each and solve them cold.
- Start a move notebook with one page per question type.
- Put two unseen papers aside and leave them closed until the final month.
- When you run out of fresh papers, the year-wise papers on DMocks are one way to keep the rhythm going.
