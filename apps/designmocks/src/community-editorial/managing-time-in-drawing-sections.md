Ask students what went wrong in a drawing section and a lot of the answers sound the same. "I spent too long on the first task." "I started drawing before I had an idea." "I kept erasing." "The last question was half done." None of these is about talent. They are about time, and time is the easiest part of a drawing section to fix.

This article gives you a plan you can use in UCEED Part B, CEED Part B and NID DAT Prelims, and a way to practise it.

## Know your section

Start with what you are timing.

- **UCEED Part B:** 60 minutes on paper, with two tasks, one on drawing and one on design aptitude. Check this year's brochure for the marks for each task.
- **CEED Part B:** 120 minutes, with five sketching and design tasks worth 20 marks each.
- **NID DAT Prelims:** a pen-and-paper test in English with text and visual prompts. The admit card gives the duration, so build your plan once you know it.

## Rule 1: read everything first

Spend the first two or three minutes reading every task before you draw a line. For each one, underline what it actually asks for: the subject, the view, any written part, any limit on colours or frames. Then jot a one-word first idea in the margin.

This feels like lost time. It is not. Reading everything first lets your mind work on later tasks in the background while you draw the first one. It also stops the nasty surprise of finding a task you misjudged with five minutes left.

## Rule 2: split the time by marks

Give each task time in proportion to its marks, and keep a small buffer at the end.

**CEED Part B (120 minutes, five tasks of 20 marks each)**

The marks are equal, so the time should be too. A workable split is 2 minutes of reading, 22 minutes per task and 8 minutes of buffer: 2 + 5 × 22 + 8 = 120.

**UCEED Part B (60 minutes, two tasks)**

Split in proportion to the marks shown in this year's brochure. If the two tasks carry equal marks, a workable split is 2 minutes of reading, 27 minutes per task and 4 minutes of buffer: 2 + 27 + 27 + 4 = 60.

**NID DAT Prelims (duration on the admit card)**

Work in percentages. Use about 5 per cent for reading, split most of the rest across the tasks by their marks, and hold back 5 to 10 per cent as a buffer.

## Rule 3: give every task four phases

Inside each task, split your time into four phases. A good starting ratio:

1. **Think (about 15 per cent):** decide the idea. What is the one thing this drawing must say?
2. **Thumbnail (about 15 per cent):** two or three tiny rough layouts in a corner of the sheet. Pick the strongest.
3. **Final (about 60 per cent):** the drawing itself, from big shapes to detail.
4. **Check (about 10 per cent):** re-read the task, add anything missing, label, title, tidy key edges.

For a 22-minute CEED task, that is roughly 3 minutes thinking, 3 thumbnailing, 13 drawing and 3 checking. For a 27-minute UCEED task, it is roughly 4, 4, 16 and 3.

The think and thumbnail phases are where most students cut corners, and it shows. A clear idea drawn simply usually communicates better than a vague idea drawn carefully.

## Rule 4: write your clock times down

As soon as the section starts, turn your plan into clock times and write them at the top of your rough sheet or in a margin, wherever the instructions allow. For CEED Part B starting at 10:00, that would be:

- 10:02 start task 1
- 10:24 start task 2
- 10:46 start task 3
- 11:08 start task 4
- 11:30 start task 5
- 11:52 buffer begins

Clock times are much easier to follow than "22 minutes from when I started that one". Check this year's rules on whether you may wear a watch and what timekeeping the hall provides, and practise with the same setup.

## Rule 5: the finish-line rule

When a task's time is up, take 60 seconds to make it *readable*, then move on. Darken the main outline, add the title or label the task asks for, and finish any written part in a line or two.

A complete-looking answer with less detail almost always beats a beautifully detailed half. You can come back to add detail during the buffer if time allows. Never leave a task blank to polish another: a blank earns nothing.

## Rule 6: choose your order deliberately

You do not always have to go in printed order. Check the instructions to see whether answers must be in a particular place on the sheet. If you are free to choose:

- When marks are equal, as in CEED Part B, start with the task where your idea came fastest during the first read. An early, confident task settles your nerves.
- Do not leave the task you fear most until last. Put it second or third, when you are warmed up but not tired.
- When marks differ, protect the time for the higher-marked task first.

## Speed comes from decisions, not hand speed

Most lost minutes are spent deciding, not drawing. Make the routine decisions before exam day so you do not make them in the hall:

- Which pencil you start with and which one you finish with.
- How you build a quick human figure, and its basic proportions.
- How you set up one-point and two-point perspective in under a minute.
- Your default way of shading: direction, number of tones, where you stop.
- How you lay out a page with several frames.

Practise each default until you do not think about it. Then your exam time goes on the idea.

## Drills that build time sense

- **Five-minute thumbnails:** take any prompt from an old paper and make three thumbnail compositions in five minutes. No final drawing.
- **Ten-minute finishes:** take one thumbnail and finish it in exactly ten minutes, stopping when the timer goes.
- **One full section a week:** sit a complete drawing section with your clock times written down. Afterwards, write how long each phase actually took against your plan.

The timing review is the important part. If your think phase keeps running over, practise deciding faster with thumbnail drills. If your final phase runs over, simplify your rendering defaults.

## Try this week

- Work out the clock-time plan for your exam's drawing section and write it on a card.
- Do five-minute thumbnail drills on three old prompts.
- Sit one full drawing section with the four-phase split and the finish-line rule.
- Note your planned and actual minutes for each phase and find the one that overran.
- Fix two defaults, such as figure proportions and perspective setup, and practise them daily.
- If you need prompts to practise on, the year-wise papers on DMocks include past drawing tasks.
