"Do I need coaching to crack a design entrance?" is one of the most common questions students ask. The honest answer is that it depends less on the exam and more on you: how you work, what you already have at home, and what kind of help you actually need.

This guide won't tell you that coaching is a scam or that self-study is always enough. Both can work. Both can fail. What matters is knowing what each one gives you, and what you will have to build for yourself if you go without.

## What coaching actually gives you

Strip away the advertising and a good coaching programme offers five things.

1. **Structure.** Someone else has decided what to study each week.
2. **Feedback.** A teacher looks at your drawings and design answers and tells you what to fix.
3. **Deadlines.** Classes and tests happen whether you feel like it or not.
4. **Peers.** You see other students' work, which shows you what is possible and what you are missing.
5. **Test practice.** Regular timed papers under exam-like conditions.

Notice that none of these is magic. They are all things you can arrange for yourself, with effort. The question is whether you will.

## What self-study gives you

Self-study has real advantages too.

- **Flexibility.** You study when you are sharpest, not when the batch is scheduled.
- **Focus.** You spend time on your own weak spots instead of the class average.
- **No travel.** The hours saved on commuting go straight into practice.
- **Less money spent.** A coaching programme is a serious commitment of money as well as time.
- **Independence.** Learning to plan, practise and critique your own work is good training for design school itself.

The cost is that everything coaching provides, you now provide yourself.

## Six honest questions

Answer these in writing before you decide.

1. **Do I follow a plan without anyone checking?** Think about the last month, not your intentions.
2. **Can I get feedback on my drawings from someone?** A friend in design school, an art teacher, a peer group?
3. **Do I know what each paper looks like?** Its parts, timings and marking scheme.
4. **Do I sit timed tests on my own, under real conditions?** Or do I always check answers halfway?
5. **Do I lose motivation alone?** Some people need a room full of others working.
6. **What can my family comfortably afford, in money and in my time?** Be honest, and have this conversation openly.

If you answered "yes" to the first four, self-study is likely to work for you. If you answered "no" to most of them, coaching, or a mix, may help. Question 6 sets the limits of your choice.

## If you choose self-study: the four things to build

### 1. A syllabus map

Write down every part of each paper you are sitting. Use this year's brochure for the official structure, and past papers to see what each part actually asks. One page per exam is enough.

### 2. A weekly schedule

Fix your study hours, your daily drawing time and your full-paper day. Put it somewhere you see every morning.

### 3. A feedback loop

This is the hardest part of studying alone. Here are ways to build it:

- **Self-critique with a checklist.** After every drawing or design answer, ask: Did I answer the prompt? Is my main idea clear at a glance? Are proportions believable? Is the page well used? Would a stranger understand it?
- **Compare with your past self.** Photograph your work and keep a dated folder. Every two weeks, put old and new side by side.
- **Swap with a study partner.** Find one or two people preparing for the same exam and exchange work weekly. Give each other one strength and one fix.
- **Show a non-designer.** Ask someone at home what they see in your drawing. If they can't tell what it is, the drawing is not communicating.

### 4. A test rhythm

Sit a full past paper at least every two weeks, under real timing and marking. Review it the next day. Keep a mistakes notebook, and read it before every new paper.

## If you choose coaching: how to judge a programme

Not all coaching is the same. Before you join, try to sit a demo class and ask these questions.

- **How much individual feedback will I get on drawings?** Ask to see an example of feedback given to a student.
- **How many students are in a batch?** Large batches mean less attention for your work.
- **How often are timed tests held, and how are they reviewed?**
- **Do they use real past papers and the real marking scheme?**
- **Who teaches drawing, and what do they teach?** Ask to see the kind of exercises set.

Be careful of red flags:

- Promises of guaranteed selection.
- Pressure to sign up on the same day.
- No clear answer about feedback on your own work.
- Claims about exam patterns that don't match the official brochure.

Even in coaching, your own practice decides the result. Classes give you direction. The hours you put in at home are what move your work.

## The middle path

You don't have to choose all or nothing. Many students mix the two.

- **Self-study plus a drawing class.** Prepare the objective papers alone and take a local art or sketching class for drawing feedback.
- **Self-study plus a test series.** Plan your own studies, and use a set of timed mocks for the exam rhythm.
- **Coaching for a short period.** Join for a few weeks to learn the formats, then continue alone.

## A four-week trial

If you are still unsure, try self-study properly for four weeks before you spend money.

1. **Week 1:** Build your syllabus map and schedule. Sit one full past paper.
2. **Week 2:** Follow your schedule. Draw every day. Find one study partner.
3. **Week 3:** Sit a second full paper. Swap work with your partner.
4. **Week 4:** Compare both papers and your drawing folder. Count how many planned sessions you actually completed.

If you kept to most of your sessions and can see improvement, carry on. If you skipped most of them, or feel completely lost about your drawings, that tells you what kind of help you need.
