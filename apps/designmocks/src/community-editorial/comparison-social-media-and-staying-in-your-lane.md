You open your phone for a two-minute break. Someone your age has posted a beautiful perspective sketch. Someone else says they finished all the past papers. A third person shares a mock score higher than anything you have managed. Twenty minutes later you put the phone down feeling behind, and you do not feel like studying any more.

This happens to almost everyone preparing for design entrance exams. Design is visual, so a lot of preparation is shared as images, and images are easy to compare. This article is about how to keep that comparison from steering your preparation.

## Why comparison hits so hard during prep

When you are preparing for an exam, you are uncertain about the outcome. Uncertainty makes us look for clues about how we are doing, and other people's posts feel like clues.

But they are poor clues, for a few reasons:

- **You see their best, and your everything.** People post the sketch that worked, not the ten that did not. You, on the other hand, know every bad attempt you have made.
- **You see the result, not the process.** A finished drawing hides the hours, the reference photos and the false starts behind it.
- **You do not know their starting point.** Some people have drawn since childhood or had a teacher for years. Others started a few months ago.
- **They may not be preparing for the same thing.** NID DAT, UCEED, CEED and NIFT test different mixes of skills. A strong illustration may matter differently for each.

## Notice what you are actually comparing

Next time you feel that sinking feeling, pause and ask yourself two questions:

1. What exactly am I comparing? Their finished piece against my rough practice? Their best score against my worst?
2. Does this comparison help me decide what to do next?

Often the honest answer to the second question is no. If it does help, for example you notice they shade in a way you want to learn, then use it. If it does not, let it go and get back to your own plan.

## Audit your feed

Your feed is not fixed. You choose it, even if it does not feel that way. Spend fifteen minutes this week going through the accounts and groups you follow. For each one, ask:

- Does this teach me something I can practise?
- After I see it, do I feel like picking up a pencil?
- Or do I mostly feel smaller, anxious or behind?

Keep the accounts that teach you or make you want to work. Mute the ones that mostly make you anxious. Muting is quiet and private, and you can always undo it after the exams. You do not owe anyone your attention during preparation.

Also notice accounts that post huge promises, rumours about papers or "secret tricks". These tend to raise anxiety without giving you anything you can use.

## Set rules for when and how you scroll

Willpower in the moment is unreliable. Rules set in advance work better. Try a few of these:

- **Phone out of reach while studying.** In another room if possible, or face down in a drawer.
- **A fixed scroll window.** For example, twenty minutes after your evening study session, with a timer.
- **No scrolling in the first half hour after waking or the last hour before sleep.** Those are the times when comparison stings most and affects your day or your sleep.
- **Use a timer, not a feeling.** "I'll stop when I'm bored" rarely works.

If you use your phone for study, such as reference images or solved papers, keep those in a separate folder or app so you do not drift into your feed.

## Use other people's work as study material, not a scoreboard

There is a healthy way to look at other people's sketches. Instead of asking "Am I as good as them?", ask "What can I learn from this?"

Try this when you see a piece you admire:

1. Look at it for one minute without judging yourself.
2. Name one specific thing that works: the light source, the line weight, the way the figure is placed on the page, the colour choices.
3. Spend ten minutes practising only that one thing in your own sketchbook.
4. Write a short note under your practice: what you tried and what you noticed.

This turns envy into a lesson. Over a few weeks you build a sketchbook full of small techniques, and you stop feeling like other people's work is a threat.

## Compare with your past self instead

The only comparison that tells you whether you are improving is the one with your own earlier work.

- **Date every page in your sketchbook.** At the end of each month, put a page from the first week next to one from the last week.
- **Keep a short mock log.** Write the date, the score and the biggest source of lost marks. Look at the trend over several weeks, not one result.
- **Write one line a day.** "Today I learnt…" or "Today I practised…". On bad days, reading back through these lines is a reminder that you are moving.

## Group chats and "what did you score?"

Group chats can be supportive. They can also turn every mock into a public leaderboard. If they leave you anxious, you can:

- Mute the group during your study hours and check it once a day
- Decide you will not share scores, and have a line ready: "I'm not sharing scores this month, just focusing on my mistakes"
- Share what you learnt instead of what you scored, which is more useful for everyone

A good friend will understand. You can rejoin the conversation fully after the exam.

## Staying in your lane does not mean being alone

Staying in your lane is not about cutting everyone off. It is about running your own race with your own plan. Find one or two people, a friend, a sibling, a teacher, with whom you can talk honestly about how preparation is going. Those conversations are worth more than any number of posts.

If comparison is starting to make you feel constantly low or worthless, or it is affecting your sleep or your eating, please talk to a parent, a teacher or a counsellor. These feelings are common, and talking about them is a sensible first step.

## Try this week

- Spend fifteen minutes auditing your feed. Mute anything that mostly makes you anxious.
- Set one scroll rule, such as a fixed twenty-minute window with a timer, and keep it for seven days.
- Pick one sketch you admire and practise one specific technique from it for ten minutes.
- Date every page in your sketchbook from today.
- Prepare a one-line answer for when someone asks your mock score.
