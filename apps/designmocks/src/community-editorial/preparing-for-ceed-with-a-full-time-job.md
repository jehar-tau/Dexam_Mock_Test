Preparing for CEED while working full time is less about finding more hours and more about using small ones well. You already know how to work to deadlines, manage a calendar and show up when you are tired. Those habits are your biggest advantage. This guide turns them into a plan.

Check this year's brochure for the exam date, eligibility and anything else that affects you. The plan below assumes about sixteen weeks. If you have longer, stretch the first phase. If you have less, cut it short but keep the final four weeks.

## Know the shape of the paper

CEED has two parts with very different demands.

- **Part A** is computer-based and lasts 60 minutes. It has 8 numerical-answer questions (4 marks, no penalty), 10 multiple-select questions (4 marks, partial credit, −1 for any wrong option) and 26 MCQs (3 marks, −0.5 for a wrong answer). That is 44 questions in an hour.
- **Part B** is 120 minutes: five sketching and design tasks, 20 marks each.

Two useful numbers fall out of this:

1. Part A gives you a little over a minute per question on average. Speed and judgement matter as much as knowledge.
2. Part B gives you about 24 minutes per task if you split evenly. That is a block of time you can find on most working days.

This is the key to the whole plan. A 24-minute Part B task fits into a lunch break, a quiet morning or the first half-hour after work.

## A realistic weekly budget

Aim for eight to ten hours a week. More is fine if it is sustainable, but ten steady hours beat twenty for two weeks followed by burnout.

- **Weekdays:** 45 to 60 minutes a day, four days a week.
- **Saturday:** 2.5 to 3 hours.
- **Sunday:** 2 hours, including 30 minutes of review and planning.
- **One weekday evening off from CEED every week.** Pick the evening work usually runs late, and keep it free.

### Choosing your weekday slot

Try both of these for a week each, then keep the one that works.

- **Morning slot:** 45 minutes before work. Your mind is fresh, and nothing has gone wrong yet. Good for Part B tasks and hard Part A sets.
- **Evening slot:** 45 to 60 minutes after work, after food and a short break. Good for review, lighter Part A practice and observation sketching.

Whichever you choose, put it on your calendar as a fixed meeting with yourself. Treat moving it the way you would treat moving a client call.

## A weekday block in 45 minutes

Rotate these two types through your four weekday sessions.

- **Part B day (2 days a week):** one design or sketching task in 24 minutes, then 15 minutes of review. Ask yourself whether you answered the brief, whether your sketches communicate, and what you would do differently. Keep the last few minutes for notes.
- **Part A day (2 days a week):** 20 questions in 25 minutes, then 20 minutes checking every answer and writing down why you got each mistake wrong.
- **Observation:** this happens in the small pockets of your day, as the next section explains, and in 30 minutes of drawing from life on Sunday.

## Using your commute and breaks

Small pockets of time add up across a week. Use them for things that don't need a desk.

- **Keep a pocket sketchbook.** Draw people waiting, objects on a table, the corner of a meeting room. Five-minute sketches build observation fast.
- **Look harder at your surroundings.** Notice how signs, packaging, apps and public spaces solve problems, and where they fail. Write a line about one each day.
- **Review your mistakes notebook.** Reading yesterday's notes on the train is enough to keep them fresh.
- **Do one short visual puzzle.** A few minutes of mental rotation or pattern spotting keeps that skill warm.

Don't try to do full Part A sets on a phone during a commute. Practise under conditions as close to the real test as you can.

## A sixteen-week plan

### Weeks 1–4: audit and habits

- **Week 1:** Attempt one full CEED past paper on a weekend. Part A in 60 minutes, Part B in 120. Don't worry about the score. Note what felt hardest.
- **Weeks 2–4:** Settle into your weekday rhythm. On weekends, do one Part A paper or two Part B tasks, then review.

### Weeks 5–10: build skill and speed

- Each Saturday, alternate between a full Part A paper and three Part B tasks done back to back.
- On weekdays, focus your Part A days on whatever your mistakes notebook says is weakest.
- From week 7, practise multiple-select questions separately. Mark only the options you are sure of, and check how the −1 rule would have scored you.
- From week 8, practise a skip rule for MCQs. Before you answer, ask whether you have ruled out enough to feel confident. Track how often your skips would have been right.

### Weeks 11–16: full papers and taper

- **Weeks 11–14:** One full paper every weekend, both parts, with a long review the next day. Weekdays are short drills on your weakest areas.
- **Week 15:** One last full paper early in the week. Then lighter sessions.
- **Week 16:** Only light sketching and reading through your notes. Sleep well.

## Planning leave and energy

If you can take a few days of leave, use them wisely.

- **Don't spend leave in the first month.** Save it.
- **Use one or two days for full papers** during weeks 11 to 14. Sitting the whole paper on a fresh weekday morning is very different from doing it on a tired Saturday.
- **Take the day before the exam off** if you can, so you can rest and prepare calmly.

Watch for signs that you are running too hot: skipping meals, sleeping badly, dreading every session. If you see them, drop to 30-minute weekday sessions for a week. A short pause costs far less than burning out in week 12.

## A weekly check-in

Every Sunday, spend ten minutes on these:

1. Did I do at least five sessions this week?
2. Which Part A question type cost me most?
3. Did I finish every Part B task within about 24 minutes?
4. Is my sketching clearer than it was a month ago?
5. What one thing will I change next week?
