Most students spend months preparing for the paper and almost no time preparing for the morning of the paper. That is a pity, because the morning is where a lot of avoidable stress comes from: a missing pencil, a late cab, a friend on the phone listing topics you have not revised.

You cannot control the questions. You can control the hours before them. This article gives you a routine for the night before, the first hour after waking, the journey and the minutes before the paper starts. Try it once before a mock so that on the real day it feels familiar.

## Calm starts the night before

The best thing you can do for exam morning is to make it boring. Every decision you make the evening before is one you do not have to make while nervous.

Pack your bag by early evening and check it against a written list:

- Admit card, printed, plus the photo ID it asks for
- Every item your admit card allows: pens, pencils of different grades, an eraser, a sharpener, a scale, colours if the drawing task permits them
- A spare of anything small that could break or run out
- A water bottle and a simple snack, if the centre allows them
- Anything else your admit card mentions. Read it fully, line by line, because each exam has its own rules on what you may carry.

Then plan the journey. Know the address, how you will get there and how long it takes at that time of day. Add a generous buffer for traffic and for finding the right gate. If you can, visit the centre a day or two earlier so the building is not a surprise.

Lay out your clothes. Choose something comfortable that you have worn before, with a layer you can remove if the hall is warm or add if it is cold.

Finally, stop studying new material. The night before is for a light look at your own notes, not for a topic you have never touched. A new topic at 10 p.m. rarely sticks and often leaves you feeling behind.

## Sleep: protect it, don't chase it

Aim to go to bed at your usual time, not much earlier. Lying in the dark at 8 p.m. trying to force sleep tends to make people more restless.

A few habits help many students wind down:

- Put your phone away, ideally outside the room, about an hour before bed
- Dim the lights and do something quiet: a shower, light stretching, a few pages of a book unrelated to exams
- Write tomorrow's plan on paper so it is out of your head

If you cannot sleep, do not panic about it. Rest with your eyes closed and breathe slowly. One restless night before an exam is very common, and people still manage on the day. Worrying about the lost sleep usually costs more than the sleep itself.

## The first hour after waking

Set two alarms so you are not anxious about oversleeping. When you wake up, follow a short, fixed order:

1. Drink a glass of water.
2. Wash, get ready and dress in the clothes you laid out.
3. Eat a breakfast you have eaten many times before. Exam day is not the day to try something new or heavy.
4. Spend five to ten minutes moving: a short walk, some stretches, rolling your shoulders and neck.
5. Check your bag once more against the list, then zip it and stop checking.

Keep your phone use minimal. Group chats on exam morning are full of rumours, last-minute topics and other people's nerves. Tell your friends the day before that you will be offline in the morning and will talk after the paper.

## A breathing routine you can use anywhere

Learn one breathing pattern now and practise it daily, so on exam day your body already knows it. Here is a simple one:

1. Sit with both feet flat on the floor and your back supported.
2. Breathe in slowly through your nose for a count of four.
3. Breathe out gently through your mouth for a count of six.
4. Repeat five or six times.

Many people find that a longer out-breath helps them settle and slows racing thoughts. You can do this at breakfast, in the car, in the queue at the gate and at your desk before the paper opens. Nobody will notice.

If counting feels hard, try this instead: notice five things you can see, four things you can feel (your feet on the floor, the pen in your hand), three things you can hear. It pulls your attention back to the room.

## What to read in the last hour

Make a single revision card a day or two before the exam. It should fit on one side of a page and include only:

- The marking scheme for your paper, in your own words. For example, UCEED's single-answer MCQs carry a penalty for a wrong answer, its multiple-select questions lose marks for any wrong option, and its numerical-answer questions have no penalty. NIFT's GAT takes off a quarter mark for each wrong answer and nothing for a blank. Know your own paper's rules so you do not hesitate in the hall.
- Your plan for the paper: which section you start with and roughly how long you give each part.
- Two or three reminders that help you, such as "read the whole question" or "skip and come back".

Read that card, and nothing else. Do not open new questions, solved papers or videos in the last hour.

## At the centre, before the paper starts

Arrive early, find your room and seat, and then let yourself settle. Use the bathroom before you go in. Put your things where you can reach them.

People outside the hall will be discussing what they studied and what they missed. You do not have to join in. A polite "Good luck, I'm just going to sit quietly for a bit" is enough.

At your seat, do your breathing routine once. When the instructions arrive, read them slowly, even if you think you know them. If there is a part on paper, such as the drawing and design tasks in UCEED Part B or the sketching tasks in CEED Part B, check the instructions for what materials and colours are allowed before you begin.

## If nerves spike during the paper

Nerves in the middle of a paper are normal. Have a 30-second reset ready:

1. Put your pen down or take your hand off the mouse.
2. Take three slow breaths with a long out-breath.
3. Re-read the current question from the start.
4. If it still feels stuck, mark it and move to a question you can do.

One hard question is not a sign that the whole paper is going badly. Getting a few easy marks usually brings your confidence back faster than staring at the hard one.

## When nerves feel bigger than a routine

Some nervousness is part of caring about something. But if stress before exams keeps you from sleeping for days, stops you eating properly or feels unmanageable, please do not carry it alone. Talk to a parent, a teacher or a counsellor at your school or college. Saying it out loud to someone you trust is a sensible step, not a weakness.

## Try this week

- Write your exam-morning packing list today and pin it near your desk.
- Practise the four-in, six-out breathing for two minutes every day.
- Before your next mock, run the full morning routine as if it were the real exam.
- Make your one-page revision card with your paper's marking scheme and your section plan.
- Tell your friends now that you will be offline on exam morning.
