One of the questions students ask most is some version of this: "I can't draw. Can I still get into design?" The honest answer is that design entrance tests do ask you to draw, so avoiding it is not a plan. The good news is that the kind of drawing they ask for can be learned, step by step, by almost anyone willing to practise a little every day.

This article explains what exam drawing is really for, and gives you a plan that starts from nothing.

## What exam drawing actually asks for

Most students who say "I can't draw" mean "I can't make a drawing that looks like a photograph or a painting". That is not what a design exam is testing.

Look at the exams themselves. UCEED Part B includes a drawing task. CEED Part B is made of sketching and design tasks. The NIFT CAT tests observation, concept, colour and illustration. NID DAT Prelims uses visual prompts. In all of these, drawing is a way to show an idea, explain how something works, or describe a scene. It is a tool for communication.

So the skills that matter most are these:

- **Clear lines** that say where an edge is without hesitation.
- **Believable form**, so a box looks like a box and a cylinder looks round.
- **Sensible proportion**, so a person is not taller than the door they walk through.
- **Readable composition**, so the viewer knows where to look first.
- **Enough tone** to separate objects and show light.

None of these depends on talent. Each one is a separate skill, and you can train them one at a time.

## Get rid of three myths

**"You need natural talent."** People who seem talented have usually drawn a lot, often since childhood. You are starting later, not starting without hope.

**"I need expensive materials."** A pencil (HB and 2B), an eraser, a sharpener and cheap paper are enough. A pen is useful later because it stops you from erasing.

**"I should copy photos to improve."** Copying photos can help a little, but drawing from real objects teaches you far more about form and light. Use photos sparingly.

## The daily twenty minutes

Do this every day for four weeks. It is split into short blocks so that no block becomes boring.

1. **Lines (5 minutes).** Fill half a page with straight lines between two dots. Place two dots, look at the end dot, and draw in one confident stroke. Move from the shoulder, not the wrist. Then do the same with gentle curves and with circles.
2. **Forms (7 minutes).** Draw boxes, cylinders, cones and spheres. Draw each one from a few different angles. Lightly draw the hidden back edges too, as if the object were made of glass. This teaches you how forms sit in space.
3. **One real object (8 minutes).** Pick something at home. Before drawing, ask which basic forms it is made of. A water bottle is a cylinder with a smaller cylinder on top. A mobile phone is a thin box. Draw those forms lightly first, then refine the outline, then add a little shading.

Keep every page, even the bad ones. Especially the bad ones.

## A four-week plan

Each week adds one new focus to the daily twenty minutes. Keep the line warm-up throughout.

### Week 1: confident lines and simple forms

Focus on stroke quality. Your lines should be light at first, then darker once you are sure. Avoid many short hairy strokes on top of each other. By the end of the week, draw ten everyday objects as combinations of boxes and cylinders.

### Week 2: tone and light

Make a tone scale: a strip of five boxes going from white to the darkest your pencil can manage. Then light one object with a single lamp or window, and shade it using only three tones: light, middle and dark. Add the cast shadow on the surface it sits on. Shading in simple steps is much easier than trying to blend everything smoothly.

### Week 3: people

Students fear drawing people more than anything else, so build them from simple parts.

1. Start with a stick figure that has the right proportions. A common guide is that an adult standing figure is about seven to eight heads tall. Mark the head, shoulders, hips, knees and feet.
2. Add simple shapes over the stick figure: an oval chest, a box for the hips, cylinders for arms and legs.
3. Put the figure in action: walking, sitting, carrying a bag, bending to pick something up. Show the action with the line of the spine first.
4. Add clothes last, with a few folds where the body bends.

Draw ten small figures a day this week, each in under a minute. Speed helps you capture the pose instead of fussing over details.

### Week 4: small scenes

Combine what you have learned. Draw simple scenes from your life: a person reading at a table, a vegetable seller with a cart, a bus stop. Plan each scene in a thumbnail the size of a matchbox first. Decide what is in front, what is in the middle and what is behind. Then draw it larger in about fifteen minutes.

## Habits that speed up progress

- **Draw lightly first.** Construction lines should be faint. Darken only the final lines.
- **Turn your page.** Rotate the paper so your hand moves in its most comfortable direction.
- **Step back.** Every few minutes, hold the drawing at arm's length. Errors in proportion jump out from a distance.
- **Compare with the real thing.** After every object drawing, look again and note one mistake. Fix it in the next drawing, not this one.
- **Draw small and often.** Many small drawings teach more than one large, slow drawing.

## When drawing feels discouraging

Every beginner hits a point where the drawings look worse than they did last week. This usually happens because your eye is improving faster than your hand. You can now see mistakes you didn't notice before. That is progress, even when it doesn't feel like it.

On those days, flip back to your first week's pages. Compare a box from Day 1 with a box from today. The difference is your proof that practice is working.

Also remember that the exam is not a gallery. A clear, well-planned drawing with confident lines, sensible proportions and a readable idea will usually communicate better than a beautiful but confusing one.

## A quick checklist for any exam drawing

Before you finish a drawing task, run through this list:

- Have I answered what the question actually asked?
- Is the main subject large enough and easy to find?
- Do the proportions look believable?
- Are my final lines darker and cleaner than my construction lines?
- Is there a clear light direction, with shadows that agree with it?
- Have I used the whole space sensibly, without crowding one corner?
