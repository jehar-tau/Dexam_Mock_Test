You finish a mock, the score appears, and it is far lower than you hoped. Your stomach drops. A voice in your head says you are not good enough for design, that everyone else is ahead, that you should give up.

That voice is loud, but it is not accurate. A mock is a practice run. Its whole job is to show you what is going wrong while there is still time to fix it. A bad mock that you review properly is often more useful than a good one you never look at again.

This article walks you through what to do in the hours and days after a disappointing score.

## First, give it a few hours

Do not start your review the moment the result appears. When you are upset, every mistake looks like proof that you are hopeless, and you will not think clearly about causes.

Instead:

- Close the result and step away for at least an hour
- Go for a walk, eat something, talk to someone about anything other than the mock
- Decide a time for the review: later the same evening or the next morning

The only rule is that the review must happen. Stepping away is different from avoiding it.

## Separate the score from the story

When a score disappoints us, we tend to wrap a story around it. "I'm terrible at aptitude." "I'll never be creative enough." "I wasted the whole month."

Take a sheet of paper and draw a line down the middle. On the left, write the story your head is telling you. On the right, write only facts you can check:

- My score was this
- I attempted this many questions and left this many
- I ran out of time in this section
- I lost this many marks to wrong answers

Facts are smaller and more manageable than stories. You cannot fix "I'm terrible", but you can fix "I left the last nine questions blank because I spent too long on the first section".

## Sort every lost mark into a bucket

Now go through the paper question by question. For every question you got wrong or skipped, put it into one of these buckets:

1. **Didn't know it.** The concept or method was new to you.
2. **Knew it, slipped.** A calculation error, a misread word, a wrong option clicked.
3. **Ran out of time.** You never reached it or rushed it.
4. **Risky guess.** You guessed and lost marks to the penalty.
5. **Misread the question.** You answered something slightly different from what was asked.
6. **Drawing task incomplete.** You ran short of time, or your idea was unclear.

Bucket four deserves attention, because negative marking punishes careless guessing. In UCEED, for example, a wrong single-answer MCQ costs marks, and in multiple-select questions any wrong option costs marks even if you also picked correct ones. NIFT's GAT takes off a quarter mark for each wrong answer and nothing for a blank. Knowing your paper's rules changes how you should guess.

## Find the one or two patterns that matter

Count how many marks fell into each bucket. Usually one or two buckets hold most of the damage. That is good news, because it tells you exactly where to spend the next week.

Resist the urge to fix everything at once. A plan with ten goals usually becomes a plan with zero goals by Thursday. Pick the biggest one or two.

## Turn patterns into a small plan

Here is how different patterns turn into different actions:

- **Mostly "didn't know it":** list the topics, then spend three or four short sessions this week on just those. Learn the idea, then do ten practice questions on it.
- **Mostly "knew it, slipped":** slow down on your first read of each question. Underline key words such as "not", "except" or "all that apply". Before submitting an answer, ask yourself one checking question.
- **Mostly "ran out of time":** practise sections with a timer. Set a rule such as "if I have not made progress in about a minute and a half, I mark it and move on". Then decide your order of sections before the next mock.
- **Mostly "risky guess":** write a personal guessing rule. For example, "I only attempt an MCQ if I can rule out at least two options" or "in multiple-select questions, I only mark options I am sure of". Test the rule in the next mock.
- **Mostly "misread":** read every question twice before looking at the options.
- **Drawing task incomplete:** practise the drawing task with a timer and a plan: a few minutes for thumbnails, most of the time for the main drawing, a few minutes to check.

Write your plan on one small card. Keep it next to you during the next mock.

## Redo, don't just read

Reading the solution and nodding along feels like learning, but it often is not. Two or three days after your review, attempt every wrong question again, without looking at the solution. If you get it right, it is fixed. If not, it goes back into your list.

For the drawing task, redraw it. Give yourself the same time and try again with what you now know.

## Look at the trend, not the single dot

One mock is one data point. Papers vary in difficulty, you vary in how rested you are, and some topics suit you better than others. A single low score tells you much less than the direction of your last four or five.

Keep a simple log: date, score, time left over and the biggest bucket of lost marks. After a few weeks, look at the log as a whole. Are the same mistakes appearing less often? That is real progress, even if the score itself jumps up and down.

## Be kind in how you talk to yourself

Notice the words you use. If a friend showed you their bad score, you would not tell them they are hopeless. You would say it is one test and help them find what to work on. Try saying the same thing to yourself.

Some students find it helps to finish every review by writing one thing they did well in that mock. There is almost always something: a section you managed well, a question you would have got wrong a month ago, a drawing idea you liked.

## Talk about it

You do not have to carry a bad score alone. Telling a parent, a friend or a teacher what happened often makes it feel smaller. A teacher may also spot a pattern you missed.

If you notice that low scores are leaving you feeling low for days at a time, that you have stopped wanting to study or sleep is suffering, please talk to a parent, a teacher or a counsellor. That is a sensible thing to do, and the people around you would want to know.

## Try this week

- After your next mock, wait an hour, then sort every lost mark into the six buckets.
- Pick the one or two biggest buckets and write a plan on a single card.
- Write a personal guessing rule based on your paper's marking scheme.
- Two or three days later, redo every wrong question without looking at solutions.
- Start a simple mock log with date, score and biggest bucket.
- If you practise on DMocks, take one year-wise paper under timed conditions this week and review it with the bucket method.
