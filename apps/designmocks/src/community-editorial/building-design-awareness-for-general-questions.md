"How do I prepare for design GK?" is one of the most common questions students ask, usually followed by "is there a book that covers everything?" There isn't. The made world is too big for one book. What you can build is design awareness: the habit of noticing how things around you are designed, and a system for remembering what you learn.

This article gives you that habit and that system.

## What design awareness actually means

General awareness questions in design exams can touch many areas: everyday objects and how they work, materials and how things are made, crafts and textiles, architecture, visual communication such as logos and signs, well-known designers and design movements, and current events in design. The exact mix changes from exam to exam and year to year, so check this year's brochure and recent papers for what each exam emphasises.

Two kinds of knowledge help here:

- **Facts:** who designed something, where a craft comes from, what a material is.
- **Understanding:** why an object has its shape, why a material suits a use, why a sign is easy to read.

Facts can be crammed, but they fade. Understanding stays, and it helps you answer questions about things you have never seen before. So build understanding first and hang facts on it.

## The daily object habit (15 minutes)

Pick one object every day and write its "biography" in a notebook. Use the same five questions each time.

1. **What is it for?** Who uses it, where and how often?
2. **What is it made of?** Plastic, steel, wood, glass, fabric, clay? Can you tell how it was made: moulded, cut, woven, stitched, cast, printed?
3. **Why this shape?** Which parts are shaped by the hand, by the body, by the use, by the way it is made, or by cost?
4. **What works and what doesn't?** Is anything awkward, uncomfortable or confusing? How would you fix it?
5. **What else does the same job?** Compare it with an older version, a cheaper version, or a version from another region.

Do a quick sketch beside your notes. Over a few months, this notebook becomes your own reference on how the made world works, written in your words.

Good starting objects are things you use every day: a pressure cooker, a steel tiffin, a ceiling fan, a school bag, a plastic chair, a mobile charger, a broom, a bus ticket, a medicine strip.

## A weekly theme plan

Alongside the daily habit, give each week a theme. Spend about thirty minutes a day on it, reading and taking notes. Rotate through themes like these:

- **Materials and processes:** how plastics, metals, wood, glass and textiles are shaped and joined.
- **Indian crafts and textiles:** crafts and weaving traditions from different states, with the materials and techniques each uses.
- **Architecture and spaces:** well-known buildings in India and abroad, and how climate and culture shape buildings.
- **Visual communication:** logos, typefaces, posters, road signs, packaging, and why some are easier to read than others.
- **Designers and movements:** major design movements, what they stood for, and designers associated with them.
- **Product and technology:** how everyday devices have changed, and what drove each change.
- **Design in the news:** recent design events, exhibitions, awards and debates.

Use reliable sources: museum and gallery websites, official craft and textile portals, textbooks, and well-edited design magazines and books. When a source and a forum post disagree, trust the source, and check a second one if the fact matters.

## Turning reading into memory

Reading is not the same as remembering. Use these three tools to make things stick.

### One-page theme sheets

At the end of each theme week, make one page that sums it up. Use headings, short bullet points and small sketches. If you can't fit it on one page, you haven't sorted it yet.

### Question cards

For every important fact, write a small card with a question on one side and the answer on the other. Keep questions specific: "Which material is this craft made from?" works better than "Tell me about this craft".

### Spaced review

Review your cards on a schedule rather than all at once:

1. The day after you make them.
2. Three days later.
3. One week later.
4. Two weeks later.
5. Once a month after that.

Cards you get wrong go back to step 1. Cards you get right move to the next step. Ten to fifteen minutes of review a day is enough.

## Reading the world around you

Some of the best awareness practice happens away from your desk.

- **In a market:** notice how goods are packed, displayed and labelled. Why are some products easier to find?
- **On public transport:** notice the signs, the seats, the handles. Who are they designed for, and who do they leave out?
- **At home:** look at the oldest object in the house and the newest. What changed in material, shape and use?
- **On your phone:** notice the icons, the buttons, the way an app guides you. Where do you get confused?

Write one line about something you noticed each day. These notes often give you the best ideas for design aptitude tasks too.

## How to approach general questions in the exam

- **Read every option before answering.** Options are often close, and the right one may depend on a single word.
- **Use understanding to eliminate.** Even if you don't know the fact, you can often rule out options that don't make sense for the material, region or period.
- **Know the marking.** In UCEED, a wrong single-answer MCQ costs −0.71. In the NIFT GAT, a wrong answer costs −0.25 and a blank costs nothing. If you can rule out most options, an informed choice may be worth it; a blind guess usually is not.
- **Don't spend too long.** A general question is usually either known or not. If you can't decide quickly, move on and come back.

## Mistakes that waste time

- Collecting long lists of names and dates with no context.
- Reading many sources once instead of a few sources well.
- Leaving revision until the last month.
- Ignoring everyday objects because they seem too ordinary to be tested.
- Trusting unchecked trivia from forums and social posts.
