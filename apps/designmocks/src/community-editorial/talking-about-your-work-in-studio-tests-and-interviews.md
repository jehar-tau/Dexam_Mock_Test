After the written test, some design admissions move on to stages where you make something under observation, show your work, or talk with a panel. For NID DAT, candidates shortlisted after Prelims go on to the Mains stage, and its format is set out in each year's handbook. After CEED, institutes you apply to may run their own tests or interviews, described in their admission notices. Formats change, so always read the current year's documents before you prepare.

Whatever the format, one skill comes up again and again: explaining your work and your thinking out loud. Many students who draw well find this the hardest part. This article explains what these stages usually look for, and how to practise.

## What these stages usually assess

The details vary by institute and year, but conversations about your work generally try to understand a few things:

- **How you think.** Not just the final drawing or model, but how you got there: what you noticed, what you tried, what you rejected and why.
- **Curiosity.** Whether you ask questions about the world, notice problems and enjoy finding out how things work.
- **Clarity.** Whether you can explain an idea simply, so someone else can follow it.
- **Openness to feedback.** How you respond when someone questions a decision or points out a weakness.
- **Honesty and ownership.** Whether the work and the reasons behind it are genuinely yours.

Notice what is not on the list: having a perfect answer to every question, or using impressive design vocabulary. Clear, honest thinking matters more than polish.

## A simple structure for explaining any piece of work

When you are asked about something you made, use four parts. Practise until it feels natural, not scripted.

1. **What it is.** One or two sentences. "This is a set of sketches for a lunch box for school children."
2. **Why you made it.** The problem, the observation or the prompt that started it. "I noticed my younger cousin couldn't open her tiffin without help."
3. **How you developed it.** Two or three key steps, including at least one thing that didn't work. "My first idea used a clip, but it was hard for small hands, so I tried a sliding lid."
4. **What you would do next.** What you learned and what you would change with more time. "I'd test it with real children and look at how it can be washed."

This structure works for a drawing from a studio test, a project in a portfolio or a quick idea you sketched in the room. It shows the panel your thinking, not just your result.

Keep the first answer short, around a minute. If they want more, they will ask. A short, clear answer invites a conversation; a long speech closes it.

## Preparing your stories

Go through every piece of work you might show or be asked about, and prepare a short note for each.

- Write the four-part explanation in a few lines.
- Note one decision you are proud of and one you would change.
- Note anything you learned that surprised you.
- Keep any rough sketches or early versions that show your process, if the format allows you to bring work.

Also prepare short, honest answers to questions that commonly come up in conversations like these:

- Why do you want to study design?
- What kind of design interests you, and why?
- Tell us about something around you that you think is badly designed. How would you improve it?
- What did you find hardest about this piece of work?
- What do you do outside your studies that feeds your interest in design?

Don't memorise word-for-word answers. Memorised answers sound flat, and they fall apart when the question is phrased differently. Instead, know your key points and examples, and let the words come fresh.

## Practising out loud

Thinking an answer in your head is not the same as saying it. Speaking is a separate skill, and it improves only with practice.

### The recording drill (15 minutes)

1. Pick one piece of your work.
2. Set a timer for one minute and explain it using the four-part structure.
3. Record yourself on your phone.
4. Play it back. Note any filler words, places where you rushed, and anything unclear.
5. Record it again with those fixes.

Do this with a different piece of work each day.

### The friend panel (30 minutes, once a week)

Ask a friend or family member to act as the panel. Give them your list of common questions and ask them to add a few of their own. They should interrupt, ask "why?" and challenge your choices. Afterwards, ask them which answer was clearest and which was confusing.

Explaining your work to someone who knows nothing about design is excellent practice. If they understand it, a panel will too.

### Thinking aloud while you make

If a stage asks you to make something with people present, it helps to be comfortable working and talking at the same time. Practise by narrating as you sketch: "I'm starting with the base because it has to be stable… now I'm trying a curved handle instead." This also helps you notice your own decisions.

## Handling difficult moments

- **When you don't know an answer.** Say so honestly, then think aloud. "I'm not sure, but my guess would be…" shows reasoning, which is often what the question is really about.
- **When they criticise your work.** Listen fully, then respond with curiosity. "That's a fair point. I think I'd change it by…" Defending every choice rigidly suggests you can't take feedback.
- **When your mind goes blank.** Pause, take a breath, and ask them to repeat the question. A short silence is completely fine.
- **When you made a mistake in a studio task.** Acknowledge it and explain what you would do differently. Owning a mistake calmly can say more about you than a flawless result.

## Before the day

- Read the current year's handbook or admission notice again, especially what to bring and what is allowed.
- Arrange any work you are allowed to bring in a clear order, with your strongest piece early.
- Run through your four-part explanations once, out loud.
- Prepare one or two genuine questions of your own about the programme, in case you are given the chance.
- Sleep well. A rested mind explains far better than a crammed one.

## Try this week

- Read this year's handbook or admission notice for the stages you are preparing for.
- Write four-part explanations for five pieces of your work.
- Do the one-minute recording drill on at least four days.
- Hold one friend panel session using the common questions above.
- Practise narrating aloud while you sketch, for ten minutes on two days.
