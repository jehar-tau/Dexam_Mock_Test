UCEED Part B gives you a drawing task on paper, and CEED Part B is made of sketching and design tasks. In both, you are often asked to draw a scene, an object or a situation without any reference in front of you. Two skills carry most of that weight: perspective, which makes your drawing feel like real space, and memory drawing, which gives you something to put in that space.

This article covers the basics of both, with drills you can fit into your week.

## Perspective in plain words

Perspective is a set of rules for showing depth on a flat page. You only need a few ideas to start.

- **The horizon line is your eye level.** It is not the place where the ground ends. If you sit down, the horizon line in your drawing moves down. If you stand on a roof, it moves up.
- **Parallel lines going away from you meet at a vanishing point** on the horizon line. Railway tracks are the classic example.
- **Things get smaller as they move away,** and the spaces between them shrink too.
- **Vertical lines stay vertical** in one- and two-point perspective.

### One-point perspective

Use this when you look straight at the front of something: a corridor, a road going away from you, a room seen from its doorway. The front faces of objects are drawn flat, with true horizontal and vertical lines. All lines going into depth run to a single vanishing point.

### Two-point perspective

Use this when you see an object from a corner: a building on a street corner, a cupboard viewed at an angle. There are two vanishing points on the horizon line, one to the left and one to the right. Lines on the left side of the object run to the left point; lines on the right side run to the right point.

Keep your vanishing points far apart, often near or beyond the edges of the page. Points that are too close together make boxes look stretched and distorted.

### Three-point perspective

This adds a third vanishing point above or below for a dramatic view, such as looking up at a tall tower. It is useful to know, but one- and two-point perspective will handle most exam scenes.

## Five perspective drills

Do one of these each day, about fifteen minutes each.

1. **Box field.** Draw a horizon line and one vanishing point. Scatter twenty boxes across the page: some above the horizon, some below, some crossing it. Notice that you see the bottom of boxes above eye level and the top of boxes below it.
2. **Two-point boxes.** Repeat with two vanishing points. Then turn some boxes into simple objects: a table, a bed, a cupboard, a stool.
3. **Room from the door.** Draw your own room in one-point perspective. Start with the back wall as a rectangle, run the corners to the vanishing point, then place furniture as boxes.
4. **Ellipses.** Circles in perspective become ellipses. Draw a row of cylinders, such as glasses or buckets, from above eye level down to below it. The ellipse gets rounder the further it is from your eye level.
5. **People at a distance.** Draw a horizon line, then a street scene with people standing on flat ground. If you are standing too and everyone is roughly your height, their heads sit near the horizon line wherever they stand. Only their size changes. This one trick fixes most "floating people" problems.

## Memory drawing: building a visual library

Memory drawing feels impossible when your mind goes blank in front of a prompt like "a busy railway platform". The fix is not more imagination. It is a visual library: a store of objects, people and settings you have drawn so often that you can produce them without a reference.

### Step 1: list your library

Make a list of things that often appear in everyday Indian scenes. For example:

- **People:** walking, sitting, carrying, cooking, talking on a phone, riding a cycle.
- **Vehicles:** cycle, scooter, auto-rickshaw, bus, car.
- **Street objects:** lamp post, bench, handcart, stall, signboard, water tank.
- **Indoor objects:** chair, table, fan, cupboard, bed, kitchen shelf.
- **Nature:** trees of different shapes, potted plants, clouds, a dog, birds.

### Step 2: draw from life, then from memory

For each item on the list, use a two-day cycle.

1. **Day one:** draw it from life or from several references for ten minutes. Draw it from at least three angles. Notice the basic forms it is built from.
2. **Day two:** draw it again from memory, without looking, in five minutes. Then check against a reference and correct what you got wrong.

After a few rounds, the item is in your library. Tick it off and move on. Two or three items a day will build a strong library in a few weeks.

### Step 3: simplify each item into a formula

For every item, find a quick formula you can repeat. A scooter might be "two small wheels, a low curved body, a handlebar post, a seat box". An auto-rickshaw might be "a three-wheeled base with a rounded canopy on top". These formulas are what you reach for when the exam clock is running.

## Putting a scene together

When you face a memory drawing prompt, follow the same order every time.

1. **Read the prompt twice.** Underline the place, the time, the people and any action or mood it asks for.
2. **Thumbnail three options (3–5 minutes).** Small boxes, rough marks. Try a different viewpoint in each: eye level, high angle, low angle.
3. **Choose one and set the horizon line.** Decide where the vanishing points go.
4. **Block in the big shapes.** Buildings, ground plane, the main objects, all as boxes.
5. **Add foreground, middle ground and background.** Something large and close in front, the main action in the middle, smaller detail behind. Overlap things to show depth.
6. **Place people,** using the horizon line to keep their sizes right.
7. **Add details from your library,** then light and shade with one clear light direction.

Spend most of your effort on the main subject the prompt asks for. A well-drawn central action with a simple background reads better than an evenly detailed page.

## Common mistakes to watch for

- Horizon line drawn without deciding where the viewer is.
- Vanishing points too close together, which distorts every box.
- People of the same height drawn at different head levels on flat ground.
- Everything placed in one flat row with no overlap.
- Tiny drawings that leave most of the page empty.
- Shadows that point in different directions.

- Write your visual library list and add at least ten items to it.
