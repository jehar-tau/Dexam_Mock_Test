This article is for parents and guardians. If your son or daughter has told you they want to study design, you may feel proud, unsure, worried, or all three at once. Many families know a great deal about engineering and medical entrance exams and much less about design. That gap can make conversations tense.

You do not need to become an expert in design to support your child well. You need a clear picture of what they are preparing for, a way to talk about it calmly, and a few habits at home that make preparation easier. This article covers all three.

## Start with curiosity, not a verdict

The first conversation matters. If your child feels they must defend their choice, they may stop sharing. If they feel heard, they are more likely to come to you when things get hard.

Try asking open questions and listening to the full answer:

- What kind of design interests you? Products, communication, fashion, spaces, interfaces?
- What first made you want to study it?
- Can you show me some things you have made or drawn?
- What do you know about the exams and the courses?
- What worries you about this path?

You may find they have thought about it more than you expected. You may also find gaps, which you can then explore together. Either way, you are now working on the question as a team.

## Learn what the exams actually test

A common worry is "Is my child good enough at drawing?". Drawing matters, but these exams test much more than that. Here is a short overview of the main ones:

- **UCEED** is conducted by IIT Bombay for B.Des. admissions. Part A is a computer-based paper with numerical, multiple-select and multiple-choice questions. Part B is on paper, with a drawing task and a design aptitude task.
- **CEED**, also by IIT Bombay, is for M.Des. admissions, so it is for students who already have or are finishing a degree. It also has a computer-based Part A and a sketching and design Part B.
- **NIFT B.Des.** has a computer-based General Ability Test and a Creative Ability Test on paper, which looks at observation, concept, colour and illustration.
- **NID DAT B.Des.** starts with a pen-and-paper Prelims with text and visual prompts. Shortlisted candidates go on to a Mains stage.

Together these test observation, reasoning, visual thinking, problem-solving and the ability to communicate an idea through sketches. Your child does not need to be a gifted artist. They need regular practice and clear thinking.

Details such as eligibility, dates, seats and fees change from year to year. Sit down with your child and read the official brochure or information bulletin for each exam they plan to take. Reading it together is a good way to understand the plan and to spot deadlines early.

## Your questions about the future

It is natural to ask about careers, stability and what happens if the exam does not go well. These are fair questions, and it helps to find answers from reliable sources rather than relatives or forums.

- **Careers:** ask your child to research what graduates of the courses they are considering go on to do. Institutes publish information on their official websites. Where possible, attend open days or talk to current students and graduates.
- **A backup plan:** having a second option is sensible, not a sign of doubt. Make the plan together. It might be other design colleges, another year of preparation, or a different course with design electives. Agreeing on it early removes a lot of fear from exam season.
- **Your own worries:** be honest about them, but separate them from your child's ability. "I'm worried because I don't know this field well" is easier to hear than "I don't think you can do this".

## What helpful support looks like day to day

Small, practical things at home make a large difference over months of preparation:

- **A place to work.** A table with good light and room to spread out paper. Drawing needs more space than a textbook.
- **Materials.** Pencils, erasers, paper, colours. Ask your child what they need instead of guessing.
- **Protected time.** Help them keep regular study blocks free from errands where possible, especially close to exams.
- **Sleep and meals.** Regular sleep and meals support steady preparation better than late-night cramming.
- **Trust.** Checking on them every hour can feel like monitoring. Agree on a weekly check-in instead, where they tell you what they did and what is next.

## How to talk about mock scores

Your child will take practice tests, and the scores will go up and down. This is normal. Papers vary in difficulty, and mocks are meant to expose weaknesses.

Instead of "What did you score?", try:

- What did you learn from this one?
- Which part went better than last time?
- What will you work on this week?

Please avoid comparing their scores with a cousin, a neighbour's child or a friend. Different students start from different places and prepare for different exams. Comparison rarely motivates. More often, it makes a young person hide their results.

## Balancing school, boards and design prep

Many students prepare for design exams alongside Class 12 and board exams. That is a heavy load. You can help by:

- Sitting with them once a week to plan the coming days on paper, including school, coaching or self-study, rest and family commitments
- Being flexible about family events during the busiest weeks
- Noticing when the plan is too full, and helping them cut something rather than adding more

## Exam day and results season

On exam days, help with logistics: travel, timing and a calm, ordinary breakfast. Avoid quizzing them in the morning. After the paper, let them decide whether to talk about it. A detailed review in the car on the way home can wait.

When results arrive, be ready for both outcomes. If it goes well, celebrate. If it does not, remind them that one result does not define them, and return to the backup plan you made together. Give them a few days before discussing next steps.

## Look after your child, and yourself

Preparation is stressful for students and for parents. Watch gently for signs that your child is struggling: big changes in sleep or appetite, pulling away from friends and family, or seeming unusually low for a long time. If you notice these, talk to them calmly and without judgement. If the stress seems unmanageable, involve a teacher or a school or college counsellor. Seeking help early is a sensible step.

Talk to someone yourself, too. Another parent, a friend or a counsellor can help you hold your own worries so they do not land on your child.

## Try this week at home

- Ask your child to show you three pieces of their work and tell you what they were trying to do.
- Read the official brochure for one exam together and note the key dates.
- Agree on a weekly ten-minute check-in instead of daily questions about studying.
- After their next mock, ask "What did you learn?" before asking about the score.
- Talk through a backup plan together, calmly, before exam season begins.
