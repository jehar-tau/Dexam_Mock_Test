"Should I guess or leave it?" is one of the most common questions students ask about design entrance exams. The honest answer is that it depends on the marking scheme and on how much you know. The good news is that the maths is simple. Once you have done it once, you can turn it into a rule you apply in seconds.

## The one idea: expected value

For any question, ask: if I answered questions like this many times, would I gain or lose marks on average?

**Expected value = (chance of being right × marks for a right answer) − (chance of being wrong × penalty)**

If the result is above zero, attempting gains you marks on average. If it is below zero, leaving it is better on average.

You can also work out the **break-even chance**, the point where attempting and leaving are equal:

**Break-even chance = penalty ÷ (marks for right + penalty)**

If your real chance of being right is above that, attempt. Below it, leave.

## UCEED single-answer MCQs: +3, −0.71

Break-even chance: 0.71 ÷ 3.71, which is about 0.19. That is a little under one in five.

With four options:

- **Blind guess:** 3 × 1/4 − 0.71 × 3/4 = 0.75 − 0.53 = about **+0.22**.
- **Rule out one option:** 3 × 1/3 − 0.71 × 2/3 = 1 − 0.47 = about **+0.53**.
- **Rule out two options:** 3 × 1/2 − 0.71 × 1/2 = 1.5 − 0.355 = about **+1.15**.

So, strictly on the numbers, even a blind guess is slightly in your favour. But +0.22 is small, and the result of any one guess swings between +3 and −0.71. It also assumes your guess is truly random. When you have no idea, you are often drawn towards a trap option the setters put there on purpose, so your real chance can be lower than one in four. Because the break-even is about one in five, a good trap can push you below it.

My rule for UCEED MCQs: **attempt once you can honestly rule out one option.** At that point the expected gain is clearly positive. Treat pure blind guesses as an optional last-minute decision, not a strategy.

## CEED MCQs: +3, −0.5

Break-even chance: 0.5 ÷ 3.5 = 1/7, about 0.14. That is roughly one in seven.

With four options:

- **Blind guess:** 3 × 1/4 − 0.5 × 3/4 = 0.75 − 0.375 = about **+0.38**.
- **Rule out one option:** 3 × 1/3 − 0.5 × 2/3 = 1 − 0.33 = about **+0.67**.
- **Rule out two options:** 3 × 1/2 − 0.5 × 1/2 = 1.5 − 0.25 = **+1.25**.

The CEED penalty is lighter than UCEED's, so the case for attempting is stronger. The same advice applies, though: ruling out one option is a much safer base than a blind pick.

## NIFT GAT: +1, −0.25, 0 for a blank

Break-even chance: 0.25 ÷ 1.25 = 0.2, exactly one in five.

Four wrong answers cancel one right one. Check how many options each question has in this year's paper, because it changes the blind-guess result.

If each question has four options:

- **Blind guess:** 1 × 1/4 − 0.25 × 3/4 = 0.25 − 0.1875 = about **+0.06**.
- **Rule out one option:** 1 × 1/3 − 0.25 × 2/3 = 0.33 − 0.17 = about **+0.17**.
- **Rule out two options:** 1 × 1/2 − 0.25 × 1/2 = 0.5 − 0.125 = about **+0.38**.

If each question had five options, a blind guess would be worth 1 × 1/5 − 0.25 × 4/5 = 0.2 − 0.2 = exactly zero.

A blind guess in GAT gains almost nothing on average, and with 100 questions in 120 minutes you have only 72 seconds each. That time is better spent on a question you can actually solve. Attempt when you can rule out an option. Leave blind guesses for the final minutes, if you guess at all.

## Numerical-answer questions in UCEED and CEED: no penalty

There is no penalty for a wrong numerical answer, so the expected value of entering something is never below zero. **Never leave a numerical-answer question blank.** If you cannot solve it, enter your best estimate before time runs out. Put "check every numerical answer is filled" into your last two minutes.

## Multiple-select questions in UCEED and CEED: 4 marks, partial credit, −1 for any wrong option

These are the trickiest, because one wrong option turns the whole question into −1, however many correct options you also marked.

Check exactly how partial credit is calculated in this year's brochure. Then use this logic:

- Marking nothing earns 0.
- Marking only options you are sure of earns partial credit, which is more than 0.
- Adding an option you are unsure about changes the bet. If it is right, you gain the step from your partial credit towards the full 4. If it is wrong, you lose the partial credit you had **and** take the −1.

As a formula, add an uncertain option only if:

**chance it's right × marks it would add > chance it's wrong × (marks you already have + 1)**

Plug in this year's partial-credit marks and you will usually find the bar is higher than for a single-answer MCQ. In practice: **mark the options you are sure of, and add another only when you would be genuinely surprised to be wrong.**

## Turn the maths into a habit

In the exam you will not be doing fractions. You need a quick label for each question:

1. **Know it:** answer.
2. **Ruled out at least one option:** answer the MCQ.
3. **No idea:** leave it for now. Come back in the final minutes only if time allows.
4. **Multiple-select:** sure options only.
5. **Numerical:** always enter something.

## Check your own judgement in mocks

All of this depends on one thing: when you think you have ruled an option out, are you right? Find out.

For your next two or three mocks, put a small mark next to every MCQ where you answered after ruling out an option. In the review, count how many of those you got right. If you are right well above the break-even chance, about one in five for UCEED and NIFT and one in seven for CEED, keep attempting them. If you are below it, your eliminations are less reliable than they feel, and you should tighten up until they improve.

Do the same for blind guesses if you take any. Over several mocks you will see whether they are earning or costing you marks.

## What the maths does not tell you

- **Time.** A guess is only cheap if it is quick. Thinking for three minutes and then guessing costs you an easier question.
- **Nerves.** A run of guesses can shake your confidence. If guessing makes you anxious, use the stricter rule: rule out one first.
- **Rules change.** Marking schemes can change. Confirm the marks and penalties in this year's brochure before your final mocks.
